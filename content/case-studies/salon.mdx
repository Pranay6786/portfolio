Three things we were confident about at launch: the location, the pricing, and how much space we needed. We were right about two.

What follows is not a growth story. It is the decision trail of a business my friend and I started in February 2023 with no operating data, no reputation and no way to test most of what we believed - and what three years of booking data went on to correct.

## We were the customers first

The gap we noticed was in our own locality in Thane. There was no unisex option at the quality level we thought the area would pay for, and we were the people wanting it.

Choosing the site was therefore not a search problem. The gap and our own homes pointed at the same place, which meant we could both be physically present through the launch months without that costing us anything - a real advantage when neither of us had run a salon before and every decision needed watching.

<Callout>
Wanting something ourselves is not evidence that anyone else does. We were a sample of two, and the thing that made us confident about the location is the same thing that should have made us cautious about everything downstream of it.
</Callout>

## What we could charge

We priced below the chains and still had empty afternoons. It took me a year to understand that price was never the thing stopping people.

The pricing work itself was thorough. We benchmarked widely before setting a single rate - the chains, Envy, Kapils, Jawed Habib and the L'Oréal salons, and the local independents around us. We landed at par with the market, and below it on services where the competing brand was the stronger name.

The reasoning holds up: a new salon cannot charge a premium it has not earned, so price was the one lever we could pull to make trying us a low-risk decision. What I got wrong was the conclusion I drew from it. Being cheaper than Kapils is only useful to someone who has heard of us, and in year one almost nobody had.

## What went on the shelf

We stocked L'Oréal, Schwarzkopf, O3+, Glam, Nashi Argan, Serenite and Wella. Costs varied widely across them and the choice was explicit: recognisable brands first, margin second.

In the launch months, a customer recognising the bottle on the shelf does more for footfall than a better unit cost does for the P&L. That was a deliberate trade of near-term margin for trust we had no other way to buy. Margins have widened each year since, in line with product costs rather than ahead of them - the plan was always to earn the spread later, once there was a repeat base to earn it from.

## Hiring with no track record

We opened with nine people: two unisex hairstylists, two hairdressers, two beauticians, a nail artist and a housekeeper.

We had nothing to recruit against. No reviews, no reputation, no reason for a good stylist to leave a full chair elsewhere. The first round came entirely through contacts at a salon academy and in the industry - we borrowed their credibility because we had none of our own. Every round after that ran on the network we built ourselves, which is the part that actually compounded.

## The lease we gave back

We opened with two units: a large forward-facing shop and a smaller one behind it.

Within six or seven months the second was barely filling while its rent arrived every month - roughly a third of what we paid in total. So we dropped it.

<Callout title="The assumption underneath it">
We had signed for that unit on a capacity assumption we never tested. We sized the space for the demand we expected rather than the demand we could demonstrate, and we paid about a third of our rent for six months to find out the difference. It is the clearest mistake I have made in the business, and the one I am most careful about repeating - every capacity decision since has waited for the booking data to justify it.
</Callout>

## Reading a salon like a product

Everything runs on DINGG: appointments, staff rosters, inventory and billing, with reports for sales, sales targets, staff, invoices, memberships, inventory, customers, expenses, forecasting and marketing.

Three years of that is the closest thing I have to a product analytics stack, and I read it the same way. Two patterns came out of it early.

The first was seasonal: revenue dipped in predictable off-season stretches. The second was structural and more interesting - weekday afternoons, most chairs sat empty while all nine staff were paid. A stylist's hour is perishable inventory. An hour nobody books is not deferred revenue, it is gone, and the cost of it lands either way.

## The membership plan

The response was a membership: a thousand rupees for a year of twenty percent off for the whole family, redeemable from the fourth visit. We did not think of it at launch - it came a month or two in, once the booking data showed us how thin repeat visits were.

We sold more than eighty in the first month of offering it.

For a salon with no reputation, that is roughly a year of committed retention per household bought at a point where we had nothing else to retain anyone with. The fourth-visit condition was the part that mattered: it meant the discount only ever applied to a customer who had already formed something close to a habit.

<Callout>
What I cannot do is attribute the revenue improvement that followed to this plan specifically. We were running happy hours and seasonal offers over the same period, and nothing in how we rolled any of it out lets me separate the effects. I know revenue improved and I know memberships sold. I do not know which one did the work, and I would not claim otherwise in an interview.
</Callout>

## What I got wrong

I thought we had a pricing problem and an off-season problem. We had a marketing problem.

We were already at or below the chains on price. Nobody walked past us and chose Envy or a L'Oréal salon because it was cheaper - they chose it because they had heard of it. Every offer we ran was an answer to a question customers were not asking, and the quiet afternoons were not a pricing failure, they were the look of a business almost nobody in the area knew existed yet.

Once we started treating it that way, the returns changed. Instagram reels do more for footfall than any discount we have run, and the consistent lesson across three years is that positioning beats campaigns: who we are for, said often enough that people recognise us, outperforms anything we have done with price.

The marketing that actually worked is still running - [Euforic on Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/euforicsalon).

## Making it run without us

The salon now runs day to day under a manager. My co-founder and I kept hiring, accounts and marketing; everything else is hers. My own time in it is two to three hours a week.

The handover was not a documentation exercise and I would not pretend it was a designed one. We introduced her to the staff, walked her through the dashboard and the reports, and let her build pace.

The friction was not the systems. It was the manager-staff relationship, which took a while to settle before it ran on its own - the staff had been managed by owners for over a year and had to be re-managed by someone who was not. Nothing broke, but nothing was smooth either, and the thing that eventually fixed it was time rather than any intervention of ours.

<Callout title="What I'd do differently">

I would test capacity before buying it. The second lease is the entire lesson: we had a way to find out whether we needed that space, which was to open without it and watch the booking data, and we chose confidence instead.

I would also run one change at a time. The membership plan is the most interesting thing we did and I cannot prove what it was worth, because we launched it alongside happy hours and seasonal offers and never staggered any of it. A week of separation between launches would have cost almost nothing and would have left me with an answer instead of a correlation.

And I would have treated marketing as the first problem rather than the third. We spent a year refining price for an audience that did not know we existed. The sequence should have been reversed.

</Callout>
